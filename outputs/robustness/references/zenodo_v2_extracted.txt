Abstract
This paper investigates whether combining conditional volatility, filtered market-regime probabilities, and extreme-
loss severity through regime-conditioned entropy weights improves equity-market stress measurement. An Adaptive
Composite Market Stress Index (A-CMSI) is constructed for AAPL, MSFT, and SPY from ARIMA–conditional-
varianceestimates,forward-filteredfive-stateGaussianhiddenMarkovprobabilities,androllingpeaks-over-threshold
generalized Pareto expected shortfall. An optional specification adds cross-asset correlation. A chronological
expanding-window design uses 2,765 adjusted-price observations from 2015 through 2025, monthly model refits,
and mature-label forecast calibration. Across 1,810 held-out 30-day volatility forecasts, Adaptive yields RMSE
0.10532, compared with 0.10457 for equal weighting and 0.10411 for the NoHMM ablation. The Adaptive–Equal
Diebold–Mariano test does not reject equal predictive accuracy (𝑝 = 0.217). Adaptive achieves a future 30-day
crisis ROC AUC of 0.658 and an after-close same-day crisis-detection F1 of 0.300 at an expanding 90th-percentile
signal threshold; these are distinct evaluation tasks. Rolling EVT and wavelet diagnostics characterize tails and
timescale dependence, while external-index comparisons remain exploratory because revised observations and
assumed publication lags are used. The findings support a reproducible stress-measurement framework but do not
establish that adaptive weighting outperforms simpler composites.

1. Introduction
A composite financial stress index seeks to summarize changing market conditions in a small number of interpretable
quantities. Neitherdailyreturnsnorconditionalvolatilityalonecompletelycharacterizetheseverityofadversemarket
episodes: two days with comparable estimated volatility may have different downside tail risks, and transient negative
returns do not necessarily signal a persistent crisis regime. The problem is especially important when an index is
evaluated as a potential early-warning signal rather than a historical description of episodes already observed.
Composite financial-stress measurement has substantial precedent. The ECB Composite Indicator of Systemic Stress
(CISS) aggregates market-specific measures through an explicitly correlation-sensitive construction [1]. More recent
work, including FIMSIS for Slovenia, compares alternative aggregation and risk-assessment methods [2]. These
studies motivate transparent benchmark comparisons and caution against assuming that additional model complexity
improves detection or prediction.
ThepresentstudyevaluatesanAdaptiveCompositeMarketStressIndex(A-CMSI)forarestrictedU.S.equityuniverse
comprising Apple (AAPL), Microsoft (MSFT), and the SPDR S&P 500 ETF Trust (SPY). The core index combines
three separately estimated components: a conditional-volatility signal𝑉, a filtered adverse-regime probability𝑃, and
anextreme-lossexpected-shortfallsignal 𝑇. TrailingShannonentropydeterminesbaselineactivityweights, whichare
altered by predefined multipliers depending on the expected HMM risk rank. A four-component variant additionally
incorporates mean rolling pairwise correlations𝐶.
The main empirical question is not whether GARCH, HMM, or EVT methods are individually new, or whether an
index can visually highlight the COVID-19 crash. Rather, it asks whether an adaptive combination yields useful

2. Related literature and contribution
2.1 Conditional risk, regime switching, and extreme tails
Time-varying conditional variance is rooted in ARCH and GARCH modeling [3, 4], with asymmetric extensions
such as EGARCH designed to accommodate leverage-type effects [5]. Latent-state approaches to economic time
series offer a probabilistic description of changing regimes [6]. In market-risk applications these models may capture
different aspects of stress, but latent-state posterior probabilities must be handled cautiously when used in historical
backtests: a smoothed probability conditions on future observations and differs from the information available at the
historical close.
Tail-riskestimationusingpeaksoverthresholddrawsonextreme-orderasymptotics[7]. McNeilandFrey[8]combine
GARCH filtering with extreme-value estimates for conditional VaR and expected shortfall. Salhi et al. [9] explicitly
combine HMM and EVT in a regime-switching VaR model. Consequently, the pairing of HMM and EVT is an
established research direction; the distinction investigated here lies in using them as separable, normalized inputs to a
dated composite index with a regime-conditioned weighting mechanism.
2.2 Stress-index aggregation and testable contribution
Entropyisaninformation-theoreticfunctional[10],butusingnormalizedentropydirectlyasaweightisnotequivalent
to the conventional entropy-deficit weighting1 − 𝐻. Here, higher temporal entropy receives greater baseline weight,
which favors indicators showing more distributed absolute activity across the trailing window rather than isolated
spikes. This is a declared operational design choice, not an inherently optimal principle. PCA and equal-weight
comparators are retained to determine whether the intended adaptivity adds value.
The contribution is therefore a reproducible implementation and chronological comparison of a specific𝑉–𝑃–𝑇 index
rather than a new theorem. The study makes three distinctions explicit: (i) multiple stress dimensions are estimated
separately; (ii) regime-dependent weight changes use only forward-filtered probabilities in held-out periods; and (iii)
component ablations and paired comparisons are allowed to favor a simpler composite.

4.5 Benchmarks and multiscale description
ThecomparisonsareEqual,PCA,Entropy-only,NoEVT,NoHMM,NoGARCH,andAdaptive4. PCAusesnormalized
absoluteleading-componentloadingsfittedonlyonearlierobservations. NoEVTremovesthetailcomponent;NoHMM

Nonetheless, removal of EVT raises RMSE to 0.10678, and NoEVT also has a lower future-crisis ROC AUC of
0.5919 than Adaptive’s 0.6583. These differences are not sufficient by themselves to establish causal or statistically
significant contributions of the corresponding components.

7. Interpretation and limitations
The main negative result is informative: a regime-conditioned entropy mechanism can deliver a plausible and visually
interpretable stress index without improving the primary held-out volatility-loss metric. One possible explanation
is redundant statistical information among conditional volatility, filtered panic probability, and expected shortfall.
Another is that economics-inspired regime multipliers overfit the conceptual importance of an indicator without
aligning with the chosen predictive loss. The present experiment does not identify the mechanism uniquely; it
demonstrates that additional adaptivity must be empirically justified.
Several methodological qualifications delimit the interpretation. First, the risk universe is restricted to two large
technology stocks and a broad U.S. equity ETF containing those stocks. Second, a 252-day 90th-percentile GPD
window is sparse for stable far-tail estimation. Third, the five-state HMM and bull/panic multipliers are analyst
choices whose sensitivity was not fully explored. Fourth, scores are operationally after-close and cannot establish
intraday reaction or same-day prevention.
Fifth, future realized-volatility targets overlap, reducing the effective number of independent forecast observations.
HAC and block bootstrap procedures mitigate some serial-dependence concerns but do not resolve uncertainty
from exploratory comparison selection. Sixth, daily adjusted-price histories and external financial-stress series
may incorporate revisions unavailable at the time. Seventh, wavelet coherence is interpreted without formal Monte
Carlo significance contours. Eighth, the regime-transition target is a model-derived proxy rather than independently
observed financial-crisis ground truth.
A natural extension would compare direct-entropy weights𝐻 with entropy-deficit weights1 − 𝐻, expand to non-
overlapping asset groups and credit/funding indicators, evaluate alternative EVT windows and thresholds, obtain
point-in-time external benchmark vintages, and replicate in new periods. Such work could show whether adaptive
weighting has localized downturn benefits even when average volatility RMSE is unchanged.
8. Conclusion
A-CMSI provides a transparent, reproducible, after-close composite equity-market stress measurement experiment by
integratingforward-operationalconditionalvolatility, filteredlatent-regimeprobabilities, androllingEVTtailseverity.
Dynamic entropy weights and regime multipliers are tested against simpler aggregation and component-ablation
alternatives under chronological forecast calibration. For 1,810 scored held-out forecasts, Adaptive has RMSE
0.10532 compared with 0.10457 for Equal and 0.10411 for NoHMM; its difference from Equal is not statistically
significant in the reported test. A-CMSI retains exploratory value as a multidimensional stress summary, but the
evidence does not support a universal forecasting advantage. Broader replication and more demanding tail-risk and
point-in-time assessments should precede operational decision claims.
