Abstract
This paper investigates whether conditional volatility, ﬁltered market-regime probabilities
and extreme-loss severity beneﬁt from regime-conditioned entropy weighting in a composite
equity-market stress indicator. The Adaptive Composite Market Stress Index (A-CMSI) com-
bines ARIMA–conditional-variance models, forward-ﬁltered Gaussian hidden Markov probabil-
ities and rolling peaks-over-threshold expected shortfall for AAPL, MSFT and SPY. Fresh esti-
mation reproduces the previously reported ﬁve-state results on 2,765 adjusted-price observations
from 2015–2025 and 1,810 scored 30-day forecasts: Adaptive RMSE is 0.10532, compared with
0.10457 for Equal and 0.10411 for NoHMM. This revision executes complete three-, four-, ﬁve-
and six-state paths, independently removes panic probability and regime multipliers, evaluates
direct and deﬁcit entropy weights, and tests nine EVT window/threshold conﬁgurations. The
controlled Adaptive speciﬁcation has RMSE 0.10532 on 1810 matched dates; the lowest point-
estimate RMSE among the displayed composite/baseline variants is obtained by NoHMM. Five
EVT conﬁgurations cannot meet the ﬁxed minimum-exceedance rule and become entirely empir-
ical fallback estimates. The study separates after-close detection from future-event prediction,
reports dependence-aware uncertainty and multiplicity-adjusted loss comparisons, and retains
unfavorable baselines. Adaptive weighting has no demonstrated predictive advantage over sim-
pler weighting baselines in this sample. The evidence supports a transparent research framework,
not a deployment-ready systemic-risk indicator.
Keywords: equity-market stress; composite indicator; GARCH; hidden Markov model; extreme
value theory; entropy weighting; chronological validation.

1 Introduction
Financial stress is multidimensional. Conditional dispersion, persistent market regimes and the
severity of extreme losses need not convey identical information. A composite index can summarize
1
these signals, but a plausible economic narrative is not evidence of improved forecasting. More
elaborate aggregation can add estimation noise, obscure redundant components, or concentrate
weight on signals unrelated to the chosen loss. A stress index should therefore be evaluated against
simple alternatives without a presumption that adaptive construction must win.
Financial stress composites have substantial precedent. CISS combines market-speciﬁc indicators
while accounting for cross-market dependence [ Holló et al. , 2012]. FIMSIS examines stress aggrega-
tion in a smaller ﬁnancial system [ Drenkovska and Lenarčič, 2025]. A-CMSI has a narrower scope:
two large technology stocks and a U.S. equity ETF containing both stocks. It is an equity stress-
measurement experiment, not a replacement for multi-market ﬁnancial-system indicators. The
contribution investigated here is the explicit combination of volatility, ﬁltered panic probability
and tail severity with trailing entropy and soft regime factors.
This revision asks three empirical questions. Does adding a sixth HMM state improve generaliza-
tion despite lower initial-training BIC? Does the panic probability contribute information beyond
V/T, and are any losses associated instead with the regime multipliers? Are index conclusions
stable to feasible POT thresholds and windows, and to direct versus deﬁcit entropy? All requested
speciﬁcations are executed under a ﬁxed protocol. Historical outputs are archived, not replaced
with a winning selection.
The previous preprint already reported that NoHMM and Equal have lower point-estimate volatility
RMSE than Adaptive. The new experiments revisit the same historical period and are therefore
retrospective robustness analyses. Their chronological implementation prevents computational look-
ahead, but does not convert an already inspected period into an independent conﬁrmatory holdout.
2 Methodological context
ARCH and GARCH model conditional variance [ Engle, 1982, Bollerslev, 1986]; EGARCH repre-
sents asymmetric responses through log variance [ Nelson, 1991]. Latent Markov regimes describe
persistent distributional changes [ Hamilton, 1989]. POT tail inference is motivated by extreme-
value approximations [ Pickands, 1975], with ﬁnancial conditional-tail applications in McNeil and
Frey [2000]. Hybrid HMM–EVT risk modeling is also established [ Salhi et al. , 2016]; A-CMSI does
not claim to invent these model families.
Shannon entropy quantiﬁes concentration in a probability distribution [ Shannon, 1948]; using it as
a component weight is a design choice rather than a theorem about predictive usefulness. The direct
and deﬁcit constructions deliberately reward diﬀerent temporal patterns. Cointegration [ Johansen,
1988] and wavelet analysis [ Torrence and Compo, 1998] provide descriptive context but are not daily
forecasting inputs derived from full-sample smoothing. Forecast comparison follows the squared-
loss framework of Diebold and Mariano [1995], with the small-sample horizon adjustment of Harvey
et al. [1997] and additional block resampling.

5 Experimental design and inference
5.1 Controlled comparisons
NoProbability removes P but retains V/T regime factors; Entropy removes factors but retains P;
NoHMM removes both. This distinction prevents the no-HMM ablation from merely duplicating
entropy-only. Equal, original absolute-loading PCA, NoEVT, NoGARCH and Adaptive4 remain
visible. PCA uses earlier observations only. The original PCA method does not impose the mini-
mum ﬂoor; PCAFloor is a separately reported constrained alternative. Original entropy exports are
pre-ﬂoor weights, whereas the entropy index applies the ﬂoor. The audit does not silently rewrite
the original scores.

αridge = 1; logistic regression uses inverse regularization C = 1. They are exploratory incremental-
information diagnostics, not new composite weights. No hyperparameter is optimized on evaluation
outcomes.

A training label is admitted only if its entire 30-day future window ends before the next predicted
block. At least 126 mature operational observations are needed. Training means and current 30-day
realized volatility provide constant and persistence baselines.
A contemporaneous crisis label is one for SPY daily log return below −0.03 or trailing 20-day sim-
ple cumulative price return below −0.10. Same-day index threshold scores are after-close detection.
Separate logistic models with inverse regularization C = 1 forecast any crisis label in the next 30
trading days. Precision, recall and F1 use a predeclared probability cutoﬀ of 0.5; ROC AUC is
threshold-free. Expanding training prevalence is supplied as a probability baseline. For the Train-
Mean and RealizedVolBaseline rows, crisis probabilities use this training prevalence; those rows do
not represent separate volatility-driven crisis classiﬁers. The future ﬁltered-regime transition target
remains a model-derived proxy and does not substitute for observed crisis labels.

6.3 Incremental HMM contribution and weighting
Removing P alone gives RMSE 0.10519; removing multipliers alone gives 0.10478; removing both
gives 0.10411, compared with 0.10532 for Adaptive. The paired RMSE intervals for these three
Adaptive contrasts include zero. This pattern is consistent with redundant/noisy contributions
under this ﬁxed calibration, but it does not identify a unique mechanism or prove that HMM
information is useless. The lowest composite point estimate is NoHMM, 0.10411. Full paired
eﬀect sizes and adjusted loss-test p-values are reported rather than inferring signiﬁcance from point
rankings. Removing P and removing regime factors are diﬀerent interventions. Improvements must
be read with paired uncertainty, rather than assigned to an assumed mechanism based on a single
ranking. The conditional regressions ask whether P/stress has incremental information beyond
V/T under the same ﬁxed calibration. They do not establish structural causality or prove absence
of information when an interval contains zero. PCAFloor explicitly tests a constraint correction
while preserving the original unconstrained PCA baseline.

7 Discussion and limitations
The results distinguish construction from usefulness. A-CMSI gives an explicit multidimensional
after-close stress summary, but a model assembled from economically plausible components need
not minimize forecasting loss. Direct entropy weights reward sustained activity; deﬁcit entropy
rewards concentration. Regime factors impose analyst judgments about when tail severity matters
more. Changing these choices can redistribute the same correlated information rather than add
new signal.
The sixth state is evaluated on training likelihood, future emission density, regime diagnostics and
index forecasts separately. A lower BIC is a penalized training criterion, not a guarantee of stable
or useful states. Conversely, an interpretable ﬁve-state taxonomy is not statistical evidence of
superiority. Any eventual production choice should be checked on a genuinely new period, not
chosen from the most favorable retrospective metric in the present tables.
Tail inference is especially sensitive to feasibility. A 252-day 90th-percentile ﬁt contains only roughly
25 excesses; moving to a higher quantile can exhaust the sample rather than improve tail speciﬁcity.
13
Longer windows add observations but assume greater temporal stability. The operational fallback
is useful for keeping a signal deﬁned, yet changes the estimator’s statistical meaning. All-fallback
robustness cells should be described as empirical-tail alternatives.
Several remaining limitations constrain the claims. Adjusted closes and published stress series are
revised observations rather than veriﬁed historical vintages. The assets have overlapping economic
exposures and cover neither credit nor funding markets. Crisis episodes are few. Forecast targets
overlap and may remain dependent beyond the tested 30/60-day block choices. Bootstrap/HAC
intervals are approximate under ﬁnancial nonstationarity. HMM labels and transition targets are
estimated constructs. A small number of selected ﬁts have negative ﬁnal likelihood increments;
these are disclosed. The original log-volatility calibration is not an unbiased conditional-mean
forecast on the original scale. Weight multipliers, clipping, state ranks and exceedance rules are
design assumptions. Most importantly, the evaluation period has already been used in earlier
research and cannot support a claim of independent external validation.
17 numerical and timing tests and artifact-level checks support implementation integrity, not eco-
nomic truth of the models. A preﬁx test cannot establish that revised source prices were available
historically. Better out-of-sample results on one loss cannot establish performance for all crisis-
detection tasks. Non-rejection of equal loss cannot establish model equivalence. These distinctions
are retained in the interpretation rather than hidden in supplementary material.
8 Conclusion
This revision reproduces the original A-CMSI experiment and executes the previously untested
state-count, controlled-ablation, weighting and EVT-grid alternatives. All comparisons preserve
chronological model ﬁtting, mature-label calibration and ﬁxed hyperparameters. The contribution
is a transparent and reproducible stress-measurement framework with an explicit robustness audit,
not proof of uniformly superior adaptive forecasting. Adaptive weighting has no demonstrated
predictive advantage over simpler weighting baselines in this sample; failure to reject equal loss is not
equivalence. HMM6 has the smaller point-estimate volatility RMSE. The ﬁve-versus-six comparison
does not establish a family-adjusted volatility advantage; the RMSE diﬀerence remains uncertain.
This is not proof of equivalence. Training BIC alone does not establish out-of-sample overﬁtting.
Further work should use a new evaluation period, broader non-overlapping exposures, veriﬁed point-
in-time observations and independently speciﬁed tail/state diagnostics before operational or strong
systemic-risk claims are made.
